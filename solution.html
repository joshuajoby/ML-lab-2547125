Task 5: I used the IQR method to identify outliers because it works well even when the data is skewed and does not assume a normal distribution. Rather than deleting the extreme rows, I capped the values at the IQR upper boundary. This keeps all the other information in those rows intact while removing the distortion caused by the extreme AQI numbers.

In [ ]:
Q1 = aqi_clean["AQI"].quantile(0.25)
Q3 = aqi_clean["AQI"].quantile(0.75)
IQR = Q3 - Q1

lower = Q1 - 1.5 * IQR
upper = Q3 + 1.5 * IQR

outliers = aqi_clean[(aqi_clean["AQI"] < lower) | (aqi_clean["AQI"] > upper)]
print(f"Outliers detected: {len(outliers)}")
print(f"Upper cap: {upper:.2f}")

aqi_before_cap = aqi_clean["AQI"].copy()
aqi_clean["AQI"] = aqi_clean["AQI"].clip(lower=lower, upper=upper)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4))

sns.histplot(aqi_before_cap, bins=50, kde=True, color="salmon", ax=axes[0])
axes[0].set_title("AQI Before Treatment")
axes[0].set_xlabel("AQI")
axes[0].set_ylabel("Frequency")

sns.histplot(aqi_clean["AQI"], bins=50, kde=True, color="steelblue", ax=axes[1])
axes[1].set_title("AQI After Capping")
axes[1].set_xlabel("AQI")
axes[1].set_ylabel("Frequency")

To make them compatible, I first extracted the year from the AQI dates, then mapped each city to its state using a lookup dictionary, and aggregated both datasets to the state-year level. For AQI I took the mean per state per year, and for the crop data I summed area and production across all districts and crops for each state and year. Only after this transformation could the two datasets be meaningfully joined.

# Task 9 is a written briefing. See the markdown cell below.

Briefing for the State Environment Minister:

Our analysis looked at air quality data from cities across India between 2015 and 2020 and crop production records across Indian states from 1997 to 2015.

First, air quality is consistently worst during October, November and December every year. This is exactly when farmers burn leftover crop material after the harvest. The data shows a clear spike in pollution during these three months year after year, which makes a strong case for reducing crop burning during this period.

Second, air quality across India improved between 2018 and 2020. However, most of the 2020 improvement is likely due to the COVID-19 lockdown reducing traffic and factory activity, so it is difficult to say with certainty that government policy alone caused the change.

Third, states with higher pollution levels tend to show slightly lower crop production. The relationship exists in the data but it is not very strong, and things like rainfall, soil quality and the size of the state also influence production.

Based on this, the most direct action the government could take is to introduce restrictions on crop residue burning between October and December and offer farmers alternative ways to clear their fields.

One honest limitation is that this data cannot prove pollution causes lower yields. Factors like rainfall, irrigation and soil type are not included in this dataset and could explain the pattern just as well. A more detailed study would be needed before making any major policy decision based on the crop-pollution link.

sns.regplot(data=state_summary, x="Avg_AQI", y="Avg_Production",
            scatter_kws={"color": "steelblue", "alpha": 0.7},
            line_kws={"color": "tomato", "linewidth": 2})

for _, row in state_summary.iterrows():
    plt.text(row["Avg_AQI"] + 0.5, row["Avg_Production"], row["State_Name"],
             fontsize=7, alpha=0.75)

plt.title(f"AQI vs Crop Production by State (r = {r:.2f}, p = {p:.3f})")
plt.xlabel("Average AQI")
plt.ylabel("Average Annual Crop Production (tonnes)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

print(f"Pearson r = {r:.4f}")
print(f"p-value   = {p:.4f}")

Observation: The Pearson correlation shows a weak to moderate negative relationship between state AQI and crop production. A higher pollution level is linked to slightly lower production, but the r value is low enough that AQI alone explains very little of the variation in production. This means the relationship exists but is not strong. It does not prove that pollution causes lower yields. States differ in rainfall, irrigation, soil type and economic conditions, none of which are in this dataset, and any of these could explain the pattern.
